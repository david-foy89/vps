A burner management system, usually called a BMS, is the control that decides when fuel may flow to a burner and when it must stop. It sequences the light-off, proves that a flame is actually present, and shuts the fuel if that proof disappears. The burner, the mixer, and the vessel are not the BMS. The BMS is the supervisor.

On a production site that supervisor sits on equipment people already know: heater treaters, glycol dehydrators, line heaters, combustors, and flares. The failure mode is familiar. The flame goes out, gas keeps coming, and somebody finds out on the next round — or worse, during a relight. A BMS exists so that sequence is not left to memory and a fence charger.

## What it watches

The details change by model, but the job does not. Something has to confirm flame. On SureFire controllers, available through VPS, that proof is a flame rod, a thermocouple, or both, depending on the unit. Something has to operate the fuel valves. On a heater, something often has to hold a process temperature, which is why the BMS-300 and BMS-350 read an RTD or a thermocouple and modulate the fuel train instead of only proving a pilot.

Status matters in the field. A useful controller tells you it has power, what code it stopped on, and whether it sees a flame, without a laptop as the first step. SureFire’s controller face is built around that kind of indication.

## What it is not

A BMS does not, by itself, make a facility compliant with EPA OOOOb or OOOOc. It also does not repair a flooded firetube, bad fuel gas, or a mixer that is the wrong size. Those still have to be right. The controller can only supervise the burner it is connected to.

It is also worth separating a full controller from a pilot maintainer. The SureFire SF-50 lights and holds a standing pilot. It is the right conversation when the site needs reliable ignition and does not need temperature control. The BMS-300 and BMS-350 are the conversation when the heater itself has to be run.
